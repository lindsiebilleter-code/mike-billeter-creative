Here’s what’s actually happening to your copy.

You know your business is different. You can feel it. The way you talk about it over coffee, the way you describe it to a friend who asks what you do — there’s a specific personality in there, and it’s the reason people choose you over the competition.

Then it hits the website, and somehow it comes out sounding like every other business in your industry.

That’s not a you problem. That’s a writing problem. Most copy gets written the same way it’s always been written: pull the same words and phrases from the same bucket, smooth out anything interesting or different, end up with something that’s technically correct and totally forgettable. AI hasn’t helped — it’s made the bucket bigger and the words smoother. And it’s jacked up the forgettability a few levels.

What’s missing is somebody whose actual job is to listen to how you talk about your business, find the parts that make it sound like yours, and put those parts on the page in a way that doesn’t bury your voice.

That’s what I do.

Yes, AI can write copy. It can produce competent, professional, completely indistinguishable-from-everyone-else’s copy in about thirty seconds. Ghostwriting

$150/hour

LinkedIn posts, longer-form essays, op-eds, executive thought leadership, whatever you need written in your voice rather than mine. Hourly at $150/hour.

Here’s the thing about ghostwriting: a lot of writers can’t actually do it. Their voice is their voice, and whatever they write ends up sounding like them no matter who it’s supposedly for. That’s fine if you’re hiring a writer to sound like a writer. It’s a real problem if you’re paying someone to sound like you.

What I do differently is adjust my style to match whoever I’m writing for — your voice, your audience’s expectations, the platform you’re publishing on. The piece should sound like you wrote it on a particularly sharp day, not like someone else wrote it for you.

I scope ghostwriting hourly because the time it takes depends on how much I’m starting with (bullet points, a voice memo, a half-written draft) and how much voice-capture the piece needs. If you’re considering ghostwriting as an ongoing need, the retainer option is probably a better structure than billing piece by piece.

What I don’t do

The flip side of being clear about services is being clear about non-services. A short list, so we don’t end up two emails into a conversation that wasn’t going to work anyway.

Social media management.
I don’t run accounts, schedule posts, or write recurring social content. If you need a single post written occasionally, that can fit under blog or ghostwriting work. But ongoing social management is a different job and you want someone whose actual specialty is that.
Email platform setup and management.
I write the emails. I don’t log into your Mailchimp, set up your automations, manage your list, or troubleshoot deliverability. If you need that side of things handled, I can recommend people who do it well.
Speed-priority work where voice doesn’t matter.
If you need 500 words by tomorrow and you don’t really care how it sounds, AI will do that faster than I will, for free. I write more deliberately because the writing is the point. If the writing isn’t the point, I’m not the right hire.

A copywriting project has a few moving parts.

Here’s the order they happen in, so you know what you’re signing up for before you sign up.

  1. 01

    Step 1. You send the first email.

    Tell me what you need written, what your business does, and roughly when you need it. I’ll come back within a day or two with honest thoughts on whether I’m a good fit, a real ballpark on cost and timeline, and any questions I need answered before quoting more precisely.

  2. 02

    Step 2. We have a real conversation.

    Usually a 45–60 minute call. This is where I get to hear how you actually talk about your business — the way you’d describe it to a friend, not the way it shows up in the brochure your administrative assistant put together in 2009.

    It’s also where we get into the specifics: who you’re trying to reach, what you want them to do, what’s worked and what hasn’t, anything you’ve tried before that fell flat. This conversation is the foundation of everything I write for you, so we don’t skip it.

  3. 03

    Step 3. I send a project agreement and an invoice for the deposit.

    Once we’ve agreed on scope, I send a short written agreement covering what’s being delivered, when, and at what cost. Half the project cost is due to start — that’s how I know we’re really moving forward. (Retainers work differently; those are billed at the start of each month.)

  4. 04

    Step 4. I write the first draft.

    Depending on the project, this takes anywhere from a few days to a few weeks. You’ll know the timeline before we start. I work evenings and weekends, so you won’t hear from me daily — but I’ll send a check-in if anything comes up that I need your input on.

  5. 05

    Step 5. You review and send back consolidated feedback.

    One round of edits means one consolidated set of notes — not a string of one-off requests over two weeks. I’ll deliver a revised draft based on your feedback, and if you have more changes after that, we go through one more round.

  6. 06

    Step 6. Final delivery and final invoice.

    Once the copy is approved, I deliver it in a clean format you can use (usually Google Docs, but I can deliver in whatever format fits your workflow). The remaining 50% of the project cost is invoiced at this point. Once it’s paid, the copy is yours — full ownership, no further obligations to me.

About me, briefly.

From 2012 to 2013, my wife and I walked across the United States. San Francisco to Delaware, on foot, with packs on our backs. It took fourteen months and somewhere around three thousand miles, depending on how you count.

I bring it up because most About pages start with a job title or a credential, and I think it’s more useful to tell you something that’s actually true about how I work. I’m someone who finishes things. Long things, slow things, things that turn out to be harder than they looked at the start. That trait has shaped every project I’ve taken on since.

The work

I’ve been writing professionally for almost twenty years. National food brands (Otis Spunkmeyer, Lamb Weston, Borden, Kerry Beverages). Private banking (Loft Advisors, the private wealth division of First Dakota National Bank). Higher education (Dordt University). National non-profits (Good Samaritan Society). A long stretch as a creative director at a church, leading a team of five. Plus plenty of hired-gun freelance work over the years for clients who came to me through word of mouth.

The thread through all of it has been the same: figuring out what makes a business or organization sound like itself, and then writing in a way that makes that sound come through. Industries change. The job doesn’t.

These days I lead copywriting in-house at a global non-profit, in addition to freelance work in the evenings and on weekends. The full-time role keeps me sharp on strategic and brand-level work. The freelance practice is where I get to work directly with small and mid-size businesses — the kind of voice-driven projects that don’t fit inside a big organization’s needs.

How I think about the work

Most copy is written defensively. The writer plays it safe, picks the words least likely to offend, smooths out anything interesting, and the result is technically fine — and totally forgettable. AI accelerates the problem but didn’t create it. It’s been around longer than AI has.

I write the other way. The job is to find what’s actually true about a business and put it on the page without sanding it down to nothing. Sometimes that means asking questions the client wasn’t expecting. Sometimes it means pushing back when the obvious draft is the boring one. Usually it means listening more than writing, especially at the start of a project.

This might sound like I take the craft too seriously. I don’t. The copy serves the business, not the other way around, and I’ll happily kill a line I love if a better one takes its place. But the writing should sound like somebody wrote it. That’s the whole point.

Can we start with something small before committing to a bigger project?+

Yes, a small project is a low-risk way to see how we work together before committing to something bigger. Most of my ongoing clients started with something small and expanded from there.

What kinds of businesses do you work with?+

Almost any industry. I’ve written for national food brands, private banks, universities, and small businesses in a dozen different sectors. What actually matters is whether the business has a story and voice worth surfacing. Most do, whether they realize it yet or not. If you care that your writing sounds like your business and not like every other business in your industry, we’re probably a fit.

What do you need from me to get started?+

Not much. A conversation to talk through what you’re trying to accomplish and how you’d describe your business in your own words. Any materials you have — brand guidelines, past copy, marketing collateral, the actual products or services being written about — are helpful but not required. If you don’t have that stuff, we’ll figure it out together.

What if I don’t like the first draft?+

That’s what the revision round is for. First drafts are a starting point, not a finished product — I don’t expect any client to fall in love with the first version, though I never complain when a client tells me they wouldn’t change a word. Send me the feedback, tell me what’s not landing, and I’ll rework it. If we still haven’t nailed it after a second round, we keep going (billed hourly at that point).

Do you also do graphic design?+

No. I’ve worked with dozens of graphic designers over the years and appreciate what good design does for good writing, but I’m not a designer myself. If you need one, I’m happy to point you toward talented people I trust.
