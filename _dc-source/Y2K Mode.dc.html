HERE'S WHAT'S ACTUALLY HAPPENING TO YOUR COPY

You know your business is different. You can feel it. The way you talk about it over coffee, the way you describe it to a friend who asks what you do—there's a specific personality in there, and it's the reason people choose you over the competition.

Then it hits the website, and somehow it comes out sounding like every other business in your industry.

That's not a you problem. That's a writing problem. Most copy gets written the same way it's always been written: pull the same words and phrases from the same bucket, smooth out anything interesting or different, end up with something technically correct and totally forgettable. AI hasn't helped—it's made the bucket bigger and the words smoother. And it's jacked up the forgettability a few levels.

What's missing is somebody whose actual job is to listen to how you talk about your business, find the parts that make it sound like yours, and put those parts on the page in a way that doesn't bury your voice.

That's what I do.

A QUICK WORD ON AI

Yes, AI can write copy. It can produce competent, professional, completely indistinguishable-from-everyone-else's copy in about thirty seconds. If that's what you're after, you genuinely don't need me—and I'm not being sarcastic.

But if you want writing that sounds like your business has a personality worth paying attention to, well, that's a different thing entirely. And that's what I'm passionate about.

HOW_IT_WORKS.TXT

A copywriting project has a few moving parts.

Here's the order they happen in, so you know what you're signing up for before you sign up.

  1. 1. You send the first email.

    Tell me what you need written, what your business does, and roughly when you need it. I'll come back within a day or two with honest thoughts on fit, a real ballpark on cost and timeline, and any questions.

  2. 2. We have a real conversation.

    Usually a 45–60 minute call. This is where I hear how you actually talk about your business—the way you'd describe it to a friend, not the way it shows up in the brochure your administrative assistant put together in 2009.

  3. 3. I send an agreement and deposit invoice.

    Once we've agreed on scope, I send a short written agreement covering what's being delivered, when, and at what cost. Half the project cost is due to start.

  4. 4. I write the first draft.

    Depending on the project, this takes anywhere from a few days to a few weeks. You'll know the timeline before we start. I work evenings and weekends and will check in if I need your input.

  5. 5. You send consolidated feedback.

    One round of edits means one consolidated set of notes—not a string of one-off requests over two weeks. I'll revise based on your feedback, then we go through one more round where included.

  6. 6. Final delivery and invoice.

    Once the copy is approved, I deliver it in a clean format you can use. The remaining 50% is invoiced then. Once it's paid, the copy is yours—full ownership, no further obligations to me.

THE WORK

I've been writing professionally for almost twenty years. National food brands (Otis Spunkmeyer, Lamb Weston, Borden, Kerry Beverages). Private banking (Loft Advisors, the private wealth division of First Dakota National Bank). Higher education (Dordt University). National non-profits (Good Samaritan Society). The thread through all of it has been the same: figuring out what makes a business or organization sound like itself, and then writing in a way that makes that sound come through. Industries change. The job doesn't.

These days I lead copywriting in-house at a global non-profit, in addition to freelance work in the evenings and on weekends. The full-time role keeps me sharp on strategic and brand-level work. The freelance practice is where I get to work directly with small and mid-size businesses—the kind of voice-driven projects that don't fit inside a big organization's needs.

HOW I THINK ABOUT THE WORK

Most copy is written defensively. The writer plays it safe, picks the words least likely to offend, smooths out anything interesting, and the result is technically fine—and totally forgettable. AI accelerates the problem but didn't create it. It's been around longer than AI has.

I write the other way. The job is to find what's actually true about a business and put it on the page without sanding it down to nothing. Sometimes that means asking questions the client wasn't expecting. Sometimes it means pushing back when the obvious draft is the boring one. Usually it means listening more than writing, especially at the start.

This might sound like I take the craft too seriously. I don't. The copy serves the business, not the other way around, and I'll happily kill a line I love if a better one takes its place. But the writing should sound like somebody wrote it. That's the whole point.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

How long does a typical project take?

It depends, but here are honest ranges for the most common projects:

  • Website Copy Starter (3 pages): 2–3 weeks from kickoff
  • Website Copy Standard (5 pages): 3–5 weeks
  • Website Copy Full Site (6+ pages): 5–6 weeks, scoped after we talk
  • Email Campaign: 1–2 weeks for a short sequence, 2–4 weeks for a longer one
  • Blog Post or Article: 5–7 business days
  • Editing: usually within a week
  • Ghostwriting: varies, depending on the project
Can we start with something small before committing to a bigger project?

Yes, a small project is a low-risk way to see how we work together before committing to something bigger. Most of my ongoing clients started with something small and expanded from there.

What kinds of businesses do you work with?

Almost any industry. I've written for national food brands, private banks, universities, and small businesses in a dozen different sectors. What actually matters is whether the business has a story and voice worth surfacing. Most do, whether they realize it yet or not. If you care that your writing sounds like your business and not like every other business in your industry, we're probably a fit.

What do you need from me to get started?

Not much. A conversation to talk through what you're trying to accomplish and how you'd describe your business in your own words. Any materials you have—brand guidelines, past copy, marketing collateral, the actual products or services being written about—are helpful but not required. If you don't have that stuff, we'll figure it out together.

What if I don't like the first draft?

That's what the revision round is for. First drafts are a starting point, not a finished product—I don't expect any client to fall in love with the first version, though I never complain when a client tells me they wouldn't change a word. Send me the feedback, tell me what's not landing, and I'll rework it. If we still haven't nailed it after a second round, we keep going (billed hourly at that point).

Do you also do graphic design?

No. I've worked with dozens of graphic designers over the years and appreciate what good design does for good writing, but I'm not a designer myself. If you need one, I'm happy to point you toward talented people I trust.
